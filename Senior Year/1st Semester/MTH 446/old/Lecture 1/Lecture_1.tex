\section*{Complex numbers}

Complex numbers are created from the real number line, such that 

\begin{equation}
    z = a + bi, \quad a,b \in \mathbb{R}, \; i \in \mathbb{C}
\end{equation}

It follows that 

\begin{equation}
    (a + bi) + (c + di) = (a + c) + (b + d)i
\end{equation}

Moreover, 

\begin{equation}
    (a + bi)(c + di) = ac + adi + bci - bd
\end{equation}

This is just the standard distributive rule. You also have to introduce the complex conjugate:

\begin{equation}
    \overline{z} = a - bi, \quad z = a + bi
\end{equation}

The conjugate is $\overline{z}$. Here, then some more properties:

\begin{align}
    \overline{(z\times w)} &= \overline{z} \times \overline{w} \\
    \overline{z + w} &= \overline{z} + \overline{w}
\end{align}

Also, 

\begin{equation}
    z \cdot \overline{z} = a^2 + b^2 \geq 0
\end{equation}

Then define 

\begin{equation}
    |z| = \sqrt{z \overline{z}} = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}
\end{equation}

Also 

\begin{equation}
    z \cdot \frac{z}{|z|^2} = 1
\end{equation}

Therefore,

\begin{equation}
    z \neq 0 \implies \frac{1}{z} = \frac{\overline{z}}{|z|^2}
\end{equation}

This means that all non-zero complex numbers have an inverse. Also, 

\begin{equation}
    |zw| = |z| |w|
\end{equation}

Then, we can also think of such complex numbers geometrically, on a 2-d plane. Consider 

\begin{equation}
    \frac{z}{|z|} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}} + \frac{b}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}}i
\end{equation}

This is simiilar to seeing a cosine and a sine. In particular, 

\begin{equation}
    \cos\theta = \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}}, \quad \sin\theta = \frac{b}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}}
\end{equation}

Then, $\theta$ is the argument of $z$, denoted as $\arg(z)$. Then 

\textbf{Is this correct?:}
\begin{equation}
    z = \frac{z}{|z|}(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)
\end{equation}

Moreover, 

\begin{equation}
    e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta
\end{equation}

This is also called $\text{cis}(\theta)$. Then consider 

\begin{align}
    e^{i\theta}\cdot e^{i\phi} &= (\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)(\cos\phi + i\sin\phi)\\
    &= \cos\theta\cos\phi - \sin\theta\sin\phi + (\cos\theta\sin\phi + \sin\theta\cos\phi)i \\
    &= \cos(\theta + \phi) + \sin(\theta + \phi)i\\
    &= e^{i(\theta + \phi)} \iff \text{cis}(\theta + \phi)
\end{align}

You can also derive $e^{i\theta}$ from the taylor series expansion. I won't add that here.

Also, you can think of $z$ as 

\begin{equation}
    z = |z|e^{i\theta}
\end{equation}

Towards next class, a complex function is a function of two variables. Such that 

\begin{equation}
    f(x + iy) = u(x,y) + iw(x,y)
\end{equation}

Then define a complex derivative 

\begin{equation}
    \frac{f(z + \Delta z)}{\Delta z}
\end{equation}
